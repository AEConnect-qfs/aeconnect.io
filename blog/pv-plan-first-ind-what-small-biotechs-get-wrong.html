Building a Pharmacovigilance Plan for Your First IND: What Small Biotechs Get Wrong

Most first-time IND sponsors approach pharmacovigilance planning as a documentation exercise. It isn't. Here is what regulators actually expect and where small biotech programs consistently fall short.

DS
DJ Singh
Founder and CEO, QORO Intelligence Inc.
5 min read  ·  March 2026

After 15 years in pharmaceutical quality and drug safety across oncology, hematology, and rare disease programs, I can tell you that most first-time IND sponsors make the same five mistakes in their pharmacovigilance plans. The documents look complete on the surface. The gaps show up during inspections and, more importantly, when the first serious adverse event comes in and the team has to actually execute the plan they wrote.

Mistake 1: Treating the PV Plan as a Template Exercise

The most common mistake is downloading a pharmacovigilance plan template, filling in the blanks, and filing it with the IND. The document checks a box but describes a program that does not actually exist in the organization.

A PV plan is an operational commitment. It should describe exactly how your team will receive an adverse event report, who is responsible for triage, how cases will be documented, what system will serve as the system of record, how 7-day and 15-day reporting timelines under 21 CFR 312.32 will be tracked, and who has the authority to make the causality and expectedness determinations that drive those timelines.

If you cannot walk an FDA investigator through the actual workflow described in your plan using your actual systems and your actual team, the plan is not finished.

Mistake 2: No System of Record for Adverse Events

Small biotechs frequently handle adverse event intake through a combination of email inboxes, shared spreadsheets, and paper forms. None of these constitute a validated system of record under GAMP 5 or 21 CFR Part 11. When an FDA inspection includes a review of your ICSR handling, the investigator will ask to see an audit trail. A shared Excel file does not have one.

Your PV plan needs to designate a specific, validated system for adverse event case management. That system needs to support electronic audit trails, access controls, and data integrity controls consistent with 21 CFR Part 11. Mistake 3: Vague Reporting Timeline Language

PV plans for first-time sponsors frequently include language like "serious adverse events will be reported to the FDA in accordance with 21 CFR 312.32." That sentence is true but operationally useless.

Your plan should specify:

  • How clock-start is defined and documented for every case (Day 0 = date of first receipt of minimum valid ICSR criteria)
  • Who is responsible for making the seriousness determination and when
  • How the expectedness assessment against the current Investigator Brochure is conducted and documented
  • Who is responsible for submission and through which pathway (FDA ESG, Safety Reporting Portal)
  • What the escalation path is if the responsible person is unavailable on Day 5 of a 7-day case

Vague language around timelines is not just a documentation gap. It is an operational gap that will surface on the first expedited report your team has to process under pressure.

Mistake 4: Ignoring the Aggregate Reporting Obligations

Expedited 7-day and 15-day reports get most of the attention from first-time sponsors. Aggregate safety reporting obligations get overlooked.

Under 21 CFR 312.33, IND sponsors are required to submit an Annual Report that includes a summary of IND safety information, including a line listing of serious adverse events and a narrative summary of significant safety findings from the reporting period. If your program has global reach, a Development Safety Update Report (DSUR) under ICH E2F may also apply.

Your PV plan should address both expedited and aggregate reporting, including who is responsible for compiling the annual safety summary, what data sources feed into it, and what the internal review and approval process looks like before submission.

Mistake 5: Writing the Plan in Isolation from the Clinical Team

Pharmacovigilance plans written entirely by the drug safety or regulatory team without input from clinical operations tend to have a critical gap: they do not account for how adverse events will actually reach the safety desk from clinical sites.

Your plan needs to describe the full chain of custody from the site level to the safety database. How will site staff report an adverse event? What is the expected timeframe from site awareness to sponsor awareness? How does that interface with your clock-start definition? Are investigators trained on your specific reporting requirements, or are they relying on the general instructions in the protocol?

These questions need to be answered in the PV plan and in investigator training materials, not left to informal understanding. When an event occurs at a site at 4pm on a Friday, your team should not be improvising the intake process.

What a Defensible PV Plan Actually Contains
  • Named roles and backups for every safety function, not just job titles
  • A validated, designated system of record for all ICSR case management
  • Explicit clock-start definition and timeline tracking process
  • Expectedness assessment process tied to the current version of the IB
  • Both expedited and aggregate reporting obligations addressed
  • Site-level to sponsor intake pathway documented and tested
  • ESG account established and test submissions validated before the first report
  • Version-controlled document with a defined review frequency

The Bottom Line

A pharmacovigilance plan that cannot be executed is a liability, not an asset. Regulators are not looking for beautiful documents. They are looking for evidence that your organization understands its obligations and has built a real operational structure to meet them.
