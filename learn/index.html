Backed by published sources

Case intake is the first step the clock can see.

If the first report is late or incomplete, Day 0, case validity, and everything after them work from delayed or missing data. Six published figures below. Each one has a limit printed on the card.

Six facts, with the limits attached

These are literature and regulatory figures, not AE Connect measurements. Spontaneous-report totals are not incidence rates. A reported outcome is not proof that a medicine caused the event.

  1. Underreporting

    94%

    Median ADR underreporting

    A 2006 systematic review found a median underreporting rate of 94%, with an interquartile range of 82–98%.

    Limit. Hazell and Shakir pooled 37 studies and 43 estimates from 12 countries. Methods and settings differ. This is a 2006 literature median. It is not an official FDA or EMA rate, and it is not a rate for this year.

    Hazell & Shakir, Drug Safety, 2006

  2. When the clock starts

    Day 0

    Valid case, then 15 days

    Day 0 is the date the organization first has the minimum criteria for a valid individual case safety report. An incomplete report is followed up. Expedited reports are due as soon as possible and no later than 15 calendar days after Day 0.

    Limit. This is the ICH E2D(R1) and EU GVP Module VI rule. National rules add detail, so this card is not every country’s clock.

    ICH E2D(R1), 2025, and GVP Module VI Rev 2

  3. Manufacturer to FDA

    9.94%

    Expedited reports past day 15

    About 9.94% of roughly 1.6 million expedited manufacturer reports were not received by FDA within 15 days. Reports associated with a death were delayed more often than reports that were not.

    Limit. Ma and colleagues studied January 2004 through June 2014, and they measured the lag after the manufacturer had the information. That is not the lag from a reporter’s first email to case creation. The paper does not count deaths that followed from the delay.

    Ma et al., JAMA Internal Medicine, 2015

  4. Onset to FDA

    99–126 days known ADRs173–328 days unknown ADRs

    Unknown reactions were reported later

    For the pairs in this study, the median time from onset to the FDA report was about 99–126 days when the ADR was already known, and about 173–328 days when it was not. In their counterfactual, earlier reporting shortened time-to-signal.

    Limit. Six drug–ADR pairs only. The shorter time-to-signal is a model that assumed reports at day 15 from onset. It is not a trial of intake software.

    Hashiguchi et al., PLOS ONE, 2015

  5. Death outcome flag

    1.88 million

    Reports flagged with a death outcome

    The openFDA public extract contained about 1.88 million drug-event records with a death outcome flag. That flag marks the reported outcome. It does not establish that a drug caused the death.

    Limit. The API total was 1,883,487 records with seriousnessdeath:1. The extract’s meta.last_updated was 30 July 2026. openFDA is a public extract, not a live FAERS dashboard, and spontaneous reports are not a death toll.

    openFDA drug-event API and FDA FAERS outcomes

  6. Global database

    >40 million

    Reports in VigiBase

    VigiBase, the WHO global database of reported potential side effects, held more than 40 million reports as of February 2025.

    Limit. Uppsala Monitoring Centre figure. This is the size of a spontaneous-report database. It is not an incidence rate and it is not a count of deaths.

    Uppsala Monitoring Centre, February 2025

Why many reactions never become a report

Underreporting is not only a delay after a manufacturer already has a case. A large share of reactions never enter the system, because a patient or a healthcare professional who could send a report does not send one. The 94% median on the card above is that gap in the studies Hazell and Shakir reviewed: adverse reactions found by intensive methods that were not passed to a spontaneous reporting system. The 9.94% card is a later problem. It counts expedited reports the manufacturer already had, which then reached FDA after day 15. It says nothing about reactions that were never reported.

A 2023 update asked why healthcare professionals do not report. García-Abeijón and colleagues included 65 studies. Knowledge and attitudes still dominated. The factors named most often were:

  • Ignorance, the belief that only serious adverse drug reactions need to be reported, cited in 86.2% of the papers.
  • Lethargy, meaning procrastination, lack of interest, and other excuses, cited in 84.6%.
  • Complacency, the belief that only well tolerated drugs are allowed on the market, cited in 46.2%.
  • Diffidence, fear of appearing ridiculous for reporting a reaction that is only suspected, cited in 44.6%.
  • Insecurity, the view that it is nearly impossible to say whether a drug caused a particular reaction, cited in 33.8%.

Limit. Those percentages are the share of studies that mention a factor. They are not the share of healthcare professionals who hold it, and they are not an official FDA or EMA rate. The studies are about healthcare professionals, not a survey of patients.

The same update says that non-obligation to report, and confidentiality, have emerged as reasons in the newer studies. It does not give a percentage for either. Spontaneous reporting is largely voluntary. In many settings it is not treated as a mandatory duty for the clinician or the patient, so there is often no obligation the person treats as theirs to meet. A reaction that is never sent never becomes a case. That is a description of how these systems are set up in many places. It is not one statute that excuses every physician, everywhere, from reporting.

What late intake does to the work after it

A case is valid, and Day 0 can start, when the organization has an identifiable patient, an identifiable reporter, a suspect product, and a suspect reaction. ICH E2D and EU GVP Module VI use that gate. If one of those is missing, the report is recorded and followed up. It is not ready to go forward as a valid case.

Coding, medical review, an E2B file, and signal statistics all use the case intake produced. When intake is late, those steps start late. When intake never collects the minimum criteria, they never see a valid case. Regulators tied the expedited clock to first valid knowledge, which is why the quality of the first report matters before any safety database is opened.

That is a process description. It is not an estimate of deaths following from a late report. No source on this page provides that number, so this page does not offer one.
